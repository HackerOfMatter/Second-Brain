### 💡 **Definition: Sinking Fund**

A **sinking fund** is a **repayment plan** that requires a company (the bond issuer) to **set aside money regularly** to **retire (pay off)** part of its bond or long-term debt **before maturity**.

---

### 🧠 **Purpose**

It’s designed to **reduce default risk** by ensuring the issuer has enough funds to pay back the bondholders when the debt comes due.

---

### 🧩 **How It Works**

- The company deposits money **each year** (or at set intervals) into a special account.
    
- The funds are then used to:
    
    - **Repurchase bonds** on the open market, or
        
    - **Redeem bonds** directly from investors at a fixed price.
        

---

### 📊 **Example**

Say a company issues **$10 million** in 10-year bonds.  
Each year, it must deposit **$1 million** into a sinking fund.  
After 10 years, the full $10 million will have been set aside to repay bondholders.

---

### ⚖️ **Why It Matters**

|For the Company|For Investors|
|---|---|
|Reduces large lump-sum payment at maturity|Lowers default risk|
|Can refinance debt gradually|Increases bond safety|
|Provides flexibility to buy back bonds early|May cap potential price gains|

---

### 🧠 **Quick takeaway**

> A sinking fund is like a “savings account” for paying off debt —  
> it protects both the company and investors by spreading repayment over time.
